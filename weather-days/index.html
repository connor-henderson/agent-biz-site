Who uses this, and how

Many construction contracts already include some weather in the schedule: a monthly baseline of "normal" or "anticipated" adverse weather days. Extra time is usually allowed only for weather beyond that baseline that actually stopped critical work. Some examples:

  • Federal construction: FAR 52.249-10 lists "unusually severe weather" as an excusable delay, with written notice due within 10 days. U.S. Army Corps of Engineers contracts add a monthly table of anticipated adverse weather days (ECB 2008-23, citing ER 415-1-15).
  • Courts compare the weather the contractor met with prior years' weather at that place and time of year (Broome, 1974; Edge, 2010). That data usually comes from NOAA.
  • One state example: the Tennessee higher-education weather-delay section sets a baseline of 4 to 12 days a month, counts precipitation over 0.10 inch, and asks for "actual weather data … from nearest NOAA weather station" (Section 01 26 20). Oklahoma DOT credits adverse days above the "normal adverse weather" in the spec (directive).

Who it's for, and what they do with it:

  • Project managers and schedulers, for the monthly weather log that goes in with a pay application, and for the numbers behind a time-extension request.
  • Subcontractors (roofing, concrete, paving, site work, landscaping), to show a GC how many rain or freeze days a month really had.
  • Estimators, to check an owner's anticipated-days table against NOAA's normals before bidding. Our weather delay days explainer shows the normals for 10 large metros.
  • Event and outdoor businesses, to see whether a season was really wetter or hotter than normal.

A typical workflow: (1) set the threshold your contract uses, (2) count each month against the normal here, (3) match the counted days with your daily logs to find the ones that actually stopped critical-path work, and (4) for a dispute, order certified copies of those station-days from NOAA.

Questions

How many rain days count as a weather delay?
That's set by your contract, not by us. Many specs count a day with 0.10 inch or more of precipitation; some require the weather to stop at least half of the workday, and federal (USACE) contracts compare actual days with a table of anticipated days. Set the threshold your contract uses and we count exactly that. This is information, not legal or contract advice.
Is this the same as a certified copy from NOAA NCEI?
No. It's an uncertified count from NOAA's public data. NOAA NCEI sells Department of Commerce certified copies of station records: $168 for 1–40 pages plus $12 shipping, per its price table (checked 2026-11-02). The full report lists the exact station and days to order.
Why the nearest station and not my exact site?
NOAA records weather at stations, not at every address. We use the nearest station with data for at least 90% of your days, and print its distance from the center of your ZIP. Rain and snow can differ a lot over a few miles. If you need a site-specific opinion, hire a forensic meteorologist.
What is the "normal"?
NOAA's 1991–2020 Climate Normals: the average number of days per month that crossed the same threshold over those 30 years, at the same station we count at when NOAA publishes normals for it, otherwise at the nearest normals station within 50 km (the result names it). For a part month we scale the normal by the share of the month in your range. Not every threshold has a NOAA normal (wind, and 6 inches of snow, don't). The full method is on the methodology page.
Is a rain day the same as a delay day?
No. We count days when the weather crossed a threshold at a NOAA station. Most contracts grant time only when weather actually stopped work (often for at least half of the workday) on critical-path activities, and only beyond the baseline already in the schedule. Pair our count with your daily logs.
My contract has its own table of anticipated weather days. Is this still useful?
Yes. Compare the actual days in our monthly table with your contract's number for each month instead of NOAA's normal. The "Normal" column is there for contracts that point to historical averages, and to sanity-check a table at bid time.
The station is at an airport miles away. Will that be accepted?
That's for your contract and the owner to say. Some specs name the nearest NOAA station; some ask you to agree on a source at the start of the job. We print the distance, and the full report shows up to 3 stations side by side, so you can see whether the count holds across nearby gauges. If the site's own conditions matter, an on-site weather station or a forensic meteorologist is the stronger evidence.
NOAA's data is free. Why pay $29?
It is free, and you can get it yourself from NOAA's Climate Data Online. The free count here already does the counting. The $29 saves the rest of the work: finding a usable station, cross-checking 3 stations, filling gaps without guessing, pulling the matching normals, and producing a clean PDF, the daily CSV and the list of station-days to certify.
Will this win my claim?
We can't promise that, and nobody honest can. It gives you sourced, checkable numbers. Entitlement depends on your contract, notice, schedule impact and the owner's review. For legal questions, ask a construction attorney.

Where the data comes from

  • Daily values: NOAA NCEI Global Historical Climatology Network Daily (GHCN-Daily), read live through NCEI's Access Data Service. Values NOAA flags as failing quality control are left out.
  • Normals: NOAA NCEI U.S. Climate Normals 1991–2020, monthly "average number of days" types such as MLY-PRCP-AVGNDS-GE010HI.
  • ZIP locations: U.S. Census Bureau 2024 Gazetteer, ZIP Code Tabulation Area internal points.
