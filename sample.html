Laundromat Listing Teardown: A Hypothetical Texas Coin Laundry (Sample)

Prepared by Westworks, an AI-run research service. An AI agent wrote this report from the listing and public sources, and a second AI review, run separately from the writer, checked it. It is informational only: not an appraisal or valuation opinion, and not legal, tax, lending, or investment advice. Verify everything against the seller's records with your CPA, attorney, and lender before you make an offer. Numbers in square brackets point to the source list at the end.

Sample report on a hypothetical listing. The store, its figures, and its details are invented to show how a teardown works, and they don't describe any real business. Some details are left vague on purpose, such as the rent field's period, to show how the report handles gaps that real listings often leave. The benchmarks, sources, and census data are real. Report date 2026-10-30.

Terms: SDE (seller's discretionary earnings) is profit before the owner's pay. Manager-run cash flow is profit after paying a manager. FF&E is furniture, fixtures, and equipment (some listings write FE&E). NNN rent means the tenant also pays the landlord's property taxes, insurance, and maintenance, and CAM is common-area maintenance. Coverage is earnings divided by loan payments.

1. The short version

  • Earnings quality is the first question. The listing's $133,000 SDE is 42.9% of revenue, 1.23x the 34.8% margin of the median sold laundromat [1]. After rent ($7,100 a month, our reading of the listing; Q4), the claimed figures leave $91,800 a year for utilities, two employees, repairs, insurance, and supplies. At the 2025 survey-average utility cost (19.5% of revenue [2]), about $31,350 would remain. If both work full time at the Texas minimum wage ($30,160 a year [11]), only about $1,190 is left for repairs, insurance, and supplies. Either costs are unusually low, the staff are part-time, or the SDE is optimistic. Check it against the tax returns first (§3).
  • Price: 3.10x the seller's SDE. Sold laundromats closed at a median of 3.50x SDE in 2021–2025, with the middle half between 2.72x and 4.50x, and the 2025 average alone was 4.12x [1]. The same source says aging machines or a short lease pull multiples toward 3x [1], and this store likely has both: machines about 12 years old (the store opened in 2014) if original, and 6.7 years left on the lease. So the multiple alone doesn't show a discount. At the 3.50x median, this price corresponds to SDE of $117,857; at 4.50x, to $91,667. If verified SDE comes in at or below $91,667, the price is 4.50x SDE or more, at or above the upper quartile of sold laundromats [1].
  • Financing (illustration): with the minimum 10% down, a 10-year SBA loan at today's maximum rate costs about $58,873 a year (§4). SBA requires earnings of at least 1.25 times the payments when a new owner buys a business (an "initial acquisition") [4]. The claimed SDE covers them 2.26x before you pay yourself; at the median sold margin, 1.83x. In that median case, coverage stays at 1.25x only if you draw less than about $34K a year. So whether a lender finances this price depends on the SDE the tax returns show (our inference).
  • Other things that matter: if the machines are original, they reach the end of their 10–15-year life range around 2029 [8], and re-equipping could cost from most of the asking price to more than all of it (§5, estimate). The store takes coins and cards, so its card reports (Q6) give a partial outside check on revenue. The lease has 6.7 years left, shorter than a 10-year SBA loan, and renewal options aren't disclosed (§6).

The listing reports two employees. If both work full time, the Texas minimum wage ($7.25/hour [11]) comes to $30,160 a year, before payroll taxes, leaving about $1,190 for repairs, insurance, supplies, and any NNN charges. That's thin for a store with machines to maintain. Ask what staffing, utilities, and repairs actually cost (Q2, Q7, Q8). Keeping someone on duty for every hour a typical store is open (6 a.m. to 10 p.m. [8]) would cost about $42,340, more than what's left at average utilities.

3b. What the price assumes. At the 3.50x sold median, $412,500 corresponds to SDE of $117,857. At 4.50x, the top of the middle half, it corresponds to $91,667 [1]. If this store earned the median sold margin (34.8% [1]), its SDE would be about $107,940, and the price would be 3.82x that.

3c. Capacity check. At the survey-average 5.1 turns a day for front loaders [2], if all 36 washers are front loaders, they would bring in roughly $328K–$643K a year (estimate, using the vend and dryer assumptions below). The claimed $310K is below that range. That average isn't a ceiling: the Coin Laundry Association says washers generally run from three to eight or more turns a day [8]. Assuming all revenue is self-serve washing and drying (wash-and-fold or vending would lower these figures), the claimed revenue needs:

Every cell is below the 5.1 front-load survey average, so this check can't flag the revenue as implausible, and it can't confirm it either. 2 of the 6 cells fall below the association's three-a-day low end. Low implied turns can mean spare capacity, weak demand, or low prices. The 2026 survey averages are 5.1 for front loaders and 3.8 for top loaders (an unscientific reader poll) [2]. The vend range is our estimate, built from the survey's most common prices: top load $3.00, 20 lb $3.50, 30 lb $5.00, 40 lb $6.00 [2]. Only tax returns, bank deposits, water bills, and coin or card records can confirm revenue. The machine list and vend prices (Q3) narrow this.

3d. Water-bill check. The claimed revenue implies roughly 32,000–63,000 wash cycles a year, or about 88–173 a day (estimate, from the ranges above). With the machine list, use each model's gallons per cycle (on its spec sheet) to turn that range into gallons, and compare it with the water bills. If the bills show far fewer gallons, the revenue claim has a problem. The listing says the store takes coins and cards. The card system's reports (Q6) are an outside record of part of the revenue, which makes the tax returns easier to test; the coin share still rests on what the owner reports.

3e. Verifying revenue in a cash store. Common diligence steps (our summary, not advice): match bank deposits to the tax returns month by month; compare water use with the implied cycles (§3d); count the coin boxes with the seller over several weeks before closing; and collect any card or app records. If the revenue can't be verified, ask your attorney about the structures buyers use for that, such as a holdback or a seller note tied to verified revenue.

4. Financing (an illustration, not a quote)

  • Down payment: an SBA 7(a) purchase by a new owner (an "initial acquisition," SBA's default category) needs equity of at least 10% of total project costs [4]. That's $41,250 on the price alone, more with closing costs and working capital. Seller debt can cover at most half of the required equity [4].
  • What lenders count: debt-service coverage "must be met using historical or adjusted historical earnings from the last fiscal year-end or a two-year average," and "projections cannot be used" [4]. So the loan rests on the earnings the tax returns support, as the lender adjusts them. Income that isn't on the returns, and any growth projection, can't count.
  • Payment: financing $371,250 over 10 years [5] at the SBA's maximum variable rate for that loan size, prime (7.0% [6]) plus 3.0% = 10.0% [7], costs about $58,873 a year. That's before SBA fees, and your lender may quote less.
  • Coverage: for an initial acquisition, SBA requires earnings of at least 1.25 times the payments [4], here $73,591 a year. Lenders also account for the owner's pay or living costs, and how they do it varies (our inference).

5. Equipment

  • Age: 36 washers and 46 dryers. Sizes, models, and install years aren't listed. If the machines are original, they're about 12 years old (the store opened in 2014).
  • Life: the Coin Laundry Association's schedule is 10–15 years for front-load washers (18–50 lb) and for dryers (30–60 lb), 5–8 years for top loaders, and 10–15 years for coin changers. "This schedule will vary upon usage, sales volume and maintenance" [8]. If the machines are original, they're inside that range now, and it closes around 2029, about 3 years after a purchase now. The store's low implied turns (§3c) may stretch that.
  • Replacement exposure (estimate): one lender blog, which cites no source, says commercial washers "start at around $6,000 for a 20-lb machine" and a dryer costs "around $6,000," with larger and stacked units costing more [9]. At those prices, and if all the washers are 20 lb or larger front loaders, the washers come to $216K, and the dryers to $276K as single units, or more than $138K stacked in pairs (a stacked unit costs more than one single dryer). So re-equipping runs roughly $354K–$492K before installation. The listing's $350,000 FF&E figure is below that per-machine estimate. The same blog also puts a typical store's "equipment and installations" at $100,000 to $300,000 [9], so treat all of this as highly uncertain. Sale prices tend to reflect aging equipment: the valuation source says aging machines pull multiples toward 3x [1]. Get a distributor quote before you rely on any of this.
  • Recent work: none listed. Ask about the HVAC and the coin changers (Q8).

6. Lease

  • Term: ends mid-2033, 6.7 years from the report date, shorter than a 10-year SBA loan; renewal options aren't disclosed. Laundromats "generally occupy the retail space on long-term leases (10-25 years)" [8].
  • Financing: SBA business-acquisition loans run up to 10 years [5]. Under an earlier version of SBA's rules (SOP 50 10 7.1, per a 2024 summary [10]), the lease including borrower-only options "should" cover the loan term when equipment attached to the leased space is a substantial part of the collateral, and "must" if the lender can't get a lease assignment or landlord waiver. We haven't confirmed the wording under the current rules (SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026 [5]), so ask your lender.
  • Before closing: get an extension or new lease that runs past the end of your loan, with some extra time as a buffer [10], plus the landlord's consent to assignment, the NNN/CAM breakdown, the escalation schedule, an exclusive-use clause (no competing laundry in the center), any relocation or demolition rights, and who maintains the HVAC and roof (Q4).
  • Rent: $7,100/month (our reading of the listing's rent field, which gives no period) is 27.5% of revenue (§3a).

7. Area

To show this section with real data, we placed the hypothetical store at a public landmark, the Oak Lawn Branch Library (4100 Cedar Springs Rd, Dallas), which the Census Geocoder puts in Census Tract 6.05 [12]. In that tract, 45.5% of households rent (margin of error ±10.4 points), vs. 57.6% in Dallas, 49.2% in Dallas County, and 37.4% in Texas. Median household income is $113,554 (±$12,610), vs. $70,518, $76,547, and $78,476. There are 1.35 persons per household, vs. 2.42, 2.63, and 2.69, and 10,303 people per square mile [12]. Laundromat demand usually leans on renters and larger households (our inference), so this tract's high incomes and very small households make a weaker profile than the county figures alone suggest. Tract figures carry wide margins of error, so read them as direction, not precision.

8. Red flags to verify (not conclusions)

  • Earnings quality: after rent and average utilities, the claimed SDE leaves $31,350 for staff, repairs, and upkeep (§3a).
  • Financing: at the median sold margin, SBA's 1.25x minimum leaves about $34K a year for an owner's pay (§4).
  • Revenue evidence: 2 of 6 turn estimates fall below the industry's usual low end (§3c).
  • Equipment: if the machines are original, they reach the end of their 10–15-year life range around 2029 [8], and re-equipping could cost from most of the asking price to more than all of it (estimate, §5).
  • Lease: 6.7 years left, shorter than a 10-year SBA loan; renewal options aren't disclosed (§6).
  • Unstated periods: the revenue year.

9. Questions to ask

For the broker

  1. Two years of tax returns and P&Ls. Which years make up the $133,000 SDE, and what were SDE and revenue in each? Itemize every add-back. At 42.9% of revenue, 1.23x the median sold margin, the add-backs carry a lot of weight.
  2. 24 months of water, sewer, gas, and electric bills. Who pays water and sewer, the tenant or the landlord? After rent and average utilities, the claimed figures leave about $31,350: what did utilities actually cost?
  3. Machine list: brand, model, capacity, serial number, install year, and vend price. Are any machines newer than the store itself?
  4. The full lease and amendments. Is $7,100 the monthly rent? With 6.7 years left, will the landlord extend past the end of a 10-year loan? Escalations, assignment terms and fees, NNN/CAM amounts, an exclusive-use clause, relocation or demolition rights, and who maintains the HVAC and roof.
  5. Revenue by stream (self-serve wash, dry, wash-and-fold, vending) for three years. Is wash-and-fold offered today?
  6. What share of revenue comes through the card system? Card reports, coin counts, and bank deposits, by month.
  7. Payroll: what did the staff cost in the SDE year(s)? Are any family wages added back? What hours is the store attended? The manager-run cash flow implies $38,000 a year for the owner's labor: how many hours does the owner work?
  8. Repairs and parts spend for three years. How old are the HVAC units and the coin changers?
  9. Any planned competitor openings, rezoning, or redevelopment nearby?
  10. Why is the owner selling? Will the owner sign a non-compete?
  11. When was the last vend price increase, and what happened to revenue after it? At $4.50 a wash, the claimed revenue needs 3.3–3.7 washer turns a day (§3c).
  12. Is the equipment owned outright, or financed or leased? Any liens? What does the $350,000 FF&E figure represent?
  13. Street address or cross streets.
  14. How much of the price must be paid at closing, and what seller-financing terms would the seller consider?

For your lender

  1. How do you account for the owner's pay or living costs when you test the 1.25x coverage minimum?
  2. Is a business valuation required for this loan, who orders it, and what happens if it comes in below the price?
  3. What lease term, options, and landlord documents do you need under SOP 50 10 8.1?
  4. Does seller debt that counts toward my equity have to be on full standby (no payments), and for how long?
  5. Will you require a personal guarantee or a lien on your home, and what will the SBA guaranty fee and closing costs be in dollars?
